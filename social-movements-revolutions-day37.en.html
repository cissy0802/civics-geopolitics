Day 37
Grievances exist in nearly every society, nearly all the time. Mass action almost never does. That gap is the whole question here: from individual cost-benefit, to organization and external opportunity, to state incapacity, to what the empirical record actually says. This issue is the mirror image of Day 31 (Authoritarian Resilience) — that one asked how regimes hold; this one asks how challenges form.

1. The Logic of Collective ActionFree Riding and Selective Incentives

Mechanism

What movements pursue is usually a public good: political opening, clean air, lower taxes. Once won, those who contributed nothing enjoy it anyway, and cannot be excluded. From this, Olson's 1965 The Logic of Collective Action derives a counterintuitive conclusion — shared interests do not automatically produce shared action. My participation moves the outcome by roughly nothing, while the costs (time, risk) fall entirely on me. Free riding is the dominant strategy.

Bigger groups make it worse: per-head benefits are diluted, absence is harder to notice, monitoring is harder. So a small concentrated interest routinely outweighs a vastly larger diffuse public (see Day 16). This is textbook incentive incompatibility; the only fix is to change the payoff function, i.e. selective incentives: benefits only participants get, or penalties only non-participants bear.

Debate and Trade-offs

The Olson side: observed mass participation is not a refutation — most of it can be explained by selective incentives, very low-cost symbolic participation, or misjudged odds of success. Explaining participation as "higher consciousness" defines the cause by the outcome.

The threshold-model side (models in the vein of Granovetter 1978): real decisions are conditional — "if N people are already in the street, I'll go." A distribution of thresholds plus mutual observation makes participation jump discontinuously rather than grow linearly.

Common Misconception

"Strength in numbers." For a public good, more people raises both potential strength and the incentive to free ride; the net effect is indeterminate. What is scarce is never shared grievance, but organization that converts grievance into a cost individuals can bear.

In one line: shared interests do not produce shared action; selective incentives and mutually visible participation do. Question: if free riding is dominant, why have the largest mobilizations sometimes occurred in the riskiest environments?

2. How Movements Rise and FadeResources, Opportunity, Framing

Mechanism

The early explanation was "grievance causes protest." But grievance is close to a constant while protest is rare — a constant cannot explain a variable. Three lines of work followed:

  • Resource mobilization (McCarthy and Zald 1977): treat the movement as an organization and ask whether it can obtain money, time, skilled people and channels of communication. Its trajectory tracks resource supply, not depth of suffering.
  • Political opportunity structure (Eisinger 1973): look at elite splits, allies inside the system, the cost of repression, electoral timing. What he found was a curvilinear relationship: where the system is fully closed no one dares take to the street, where it is fully open no one needs to; protest peaks in between.
  • Framing: the same facts must be assembled into "this is unjust / it can be changed / we are the ones bearing it" before participation follows.

Debate and Trade-offs

The structural side: attributing success or failure to a leader's charisma is mostly retrospective storytelling; the same people with the same demands get entirely different results in different opportunity windows.

The agency side: an opportunity only operates once it is perceived, and it is partly created by the movement itself (demonstration effects, cross-sector coalitions, forcing opponents to take a position). "Opportunity structure" has also been criticized as concept stretching — if any favorable condition counts as an opportunity after the fact, the concept loses falsifiability.

Common Misconception

That dispersal equals failure. Effects are often lagged and scattered: the issue enters the routine agenda, professional organizations persist, participants form durable networks, the vocabulary is partly absorbed by the mainstream. Conversely, enormous volume at the peak can coincide with zero policy output. Look at institutional residue, not crowd size.

In one line: a movement is not a thermometer of grievance but the product of resources, opportunity and framing arriving together. Question: if protest peaks in "semi-open" systems, does widening participation channels increase or decrease street action?

3. The Preconditions of RevolutionState Breakdown, Structural Pressure, Cascades

Mechanism

Skocpol's 1979 States and Social Revolutions executed a shift of perspective: revolutions are not primarily "made" — the state fails first. Comparing the French, Russian and Chinese social revolutions, she identifies shared structural conditions: the old regime falls into fiscal and military crisis under international competitive pressure; elites split over the response so the state cannot extract resources from them; and agrarian social structure gives those below the capacity to act on their own.

Goldstone's 1991 Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World offers a quantifiable version: population growth raises three pressure lines at once — fiscal deficit, elite overproduction (far more aspirants to elite position than positions), and deteriorating mass livelihoods.

Kuran 1991 adds the last layer: because of preference falsification, what people say publicly diverges systematically from what they hold privately, so outsiders — including the regime itself — cannot know the true scale of opposition. Once a trigger event makes a few speak openly, others' thresholds fall one by one and a cascade forms. That is why the 1989 changes in Eastern Europe surprised nearly every observer.

Debate and Trade-offs

The predictable side: structural conditions are slow variables. Fiscal position, intra-elite competition and the loyalty base of the armed forces are all observable, so one can at least state a probability band rather than a pure black swan.

The unpredictable side: the final step from slow variable to event runs through an informational cascade, and cascades are extremely sensitive to initial conditions — under identical structures, one protest disperses and another avalanches, possibly on the on-the-spot choices of a handful of people.

Methodologically these are a division of labor, not a disagreement: structure explains risk, cascades explain timing. Conflating them turns "risk is high" into "it is about to happen."

Common Misconception

That revolutions happen at the worst moment. Tocqueville's observation in The Old Regime and the Revolution is the opposite: the eve of the French Revolution was a period of improving conditions and advancing reform. Relative deprivation theory points the same way — when expectations rise faster than reality improves, the gap is widest. The high-risk zone tends to sit mid-reform, not at the trough.

In one line: the condition for revolution is a state that has already failed; the timing is set by informational cascades — the first is estimable, the second is not. Question: if structure is measurable but timing is not, what should a "political stability assessment" actually output?

4. The Empirics of Nonviolent ResistanceParticipation Barriers and Loyalty Shifts

Mechanism

Chenoweth and Stephan's 2011 Why Civil Resistance Works coded 323 mass campaigns between 1900 and 2006 — those aiming at regime change, expulsion of foreign occupation, or secession — into the NAVCO dataset. Nonviolent campaigns achieved their objective about 53% of the time; armed campaigns about 26%.

The proposed mechanism is not moral superiority but the participation barrier: strikes, boycotts, sit-ins and resignations are open to the old, the young and the physically limited, while armed struggle screens out most of the population. Large and demographically varied participation raises the cost of repression (the people being repressed include the enforcers' own relatives), which makes loyalty shifts among security forces and economic elites the strongest predictor of success. The authors also find that even campaigns that fail in the short run are followed by markedly higher odds of a democratic system within five years.

The widely circulated "3.5% rule" is one descriptive finding within this: in that sample, no regime withstood a nonviolent campaign whose peak mobilization reached 3.5% of the population.

Debate and Trade-offs

The supporting side: the causal chain is clear and testable (low barrier → large and varied participation → rising cost of repression → loyalty shift), and it holds after controlling for regime type and repression intensity — a rare macro-political finding that replicates on independent data.

The skeptical side: the core problem is selection effects — a movement may choose nonviolence precisely because it already commands a broad social base, or faces a comparatively less brutal regime; if so the preconditions do the work and nonviolence is merely their companion. Beyond that, the boundaries of "a campaign" and the judgment of "success" both involve coding discretion.

The authors' own update matters more: the success rate peaked at roughly 65% in the 1990s and has fallen since, to roughly 34% in the 2010s. Candidate explanations include regimes learning more refined responses, rapid mobilization producing thin organization, and more campaigns carrying incidental violence. Such regularities are statistical features of a historical sample, not laws of physics.

Common Misconception

Treating 3.5% as a recipe — "hit the number and you win." The authors have repeatedly clarified that this describes a historical sample rather than prescribing a future one; peak turnout is more an indicator of the result than its cause. A movement able to put 3.5% of a population in the street usually already has cross-class coalitions, organizational durability and hesitation inside the security forces — and those are what actually operate.

In one line: nonviolence gains its edge from the scale and diversity a low participation barrier permits, which in turn erodes the loyalty of those asked to enforce. Question: from 65% down to 34% — have movements grown weaker, or has the other side learned faster?

Going Deeper

If free riding is dominant, why have the largest mobilizations sometimes occurred in the riskiest environments?
Under repression, selective incentives are supplied by tight networks — kin, religious, occupational — where exiting costs more than participating. Threshold dynamics mean that once public participation passes a point, not participating starts carrying a social cost of its own. And preference falsification means bystanders had badly underestimated how many others agreed; once that information is released, everyone revises their estimate of success upward at once. What changes is the payoff structure and the information, not courage.
Why has the success rate of nonviolent campaigns declined since the 1990s?
Still an open question, with candidates on both sides. On the challenger side: falling mobilization costs make it easier to get numbers and harder to keep organization, crowds that assemble quickly lack negotiating representatives and discipline, and widely copied tactics are more easily anticipated. On the incumbent side: the response playbook has diffused too — information environment management, targeted rather than indiscriminate handling, selective concessions to key groups, waiting a campaign out. Distinguishing the two means watching organizational density and response repertoires, not turnout.
If structure is measurable but timing is not, what should a "political stability assessment" output?
Conditional probabilities and a watch list, not a timetable. Trackable slow variables include dependence on external financing, the elite position supply-demand ratio, internal cohesion of the security forces, and the density of existing social organizations — together they describe how thick the buffer is if a shock arrives. The real discipline is writing down falsification conditions in advance: what observation would make you revise the risk downward.
Neutral, multi-perspective, no side-taking.
