Day 29
The first twenty-eight issues covered how existing institutions work. This one turns to "what comes next" — without forecasting. It takes apart four structural debates: whether democracy is really in decline, what you buy and what you give up by handing decisions to experts who never stand for election, which gap in representative government referendums and sortition each repair, and what it costs to treat institutions as objects you can test and revise.

1. The Backsliding Debate: One World, Two ReadingsThe Democratic Backsliding Debate

The Mechanism

"Democratic backsliding" does not mean a coup. It means a legally elected chief executive weakening checks through a sequence of ostensibly lawful steps — replacing court personnel, adjusting electoral rules, reshaping media ownership. Each step stays within legal form and has a defensible rationale on its own, so there is no "coup moment" to react to; scholars call this executive aggrandizement. The crucial difference from a coup is that there is no threshold: opponents permanently face the question of whether the line has been crossed yet.

And it cannot be observed directly, only measured — which means the measuring instrument shapes the conclusion.

The readings therefore diverge: on the V-Dem 2025 report's classification, roughly 72% of the world's population lives in countries it codes as autocracies, and only 29 qualify as liberal democracies — while on a coarse indicator such as "are there competitive elections," the absolute number of democracies remains near its historic high.

The Argument

The decline camp: erosion happens in the gaps between coarse indicators. Elections can stay regular and the count honest while courts are packed and districts redrawn; by the time the coarse indicator moves, elections can no longer reverse it.

The sceptics (Levitsky and Way's The Myth of Democratic Recession; Treisman's tests against long historical data): first, a baseline illusion — the early 1990s were a historical outlier, and falling back from a peak is not decline; second, mood drift in expert coding — scores can slide when the atmosphere sours even though institutions have not changed.

The trade-off: one side accepts more false positives to raise detection; the other accepts more false negatives to stay robust.

Common Misreadings

"Backsliding means elections are cancelled" — the contemporary form keeps elections, and may cite the electoral mandate as grounds for weakening checks. The other is treating different indices as one ruler: they define democracy differently, so only the trend within a single index is readable.

In one line: the disagreement is often less about what the world looks like than about which ruler is being used to measure it. Question: if raising an indicator's detection rate necessarily raises its false-positive rate, which side of the threshold would you err on?

2. Technocracy: Handing Decisions to People Who Never RunTechnocracy

The Mechanism

Technocracy is not "experts in office." It is the insulation of a class of decisions from the electoral cycle, handing them to bodies that voters neither directly mandate nor easily remove — what scholars call non-majoritarian institutions.

It solves time inconsistency: promising today not to loosen next year is not credible, because by next year loosening will look like the better move. An engineering analogy: burning a setting into read-only firmware — the fact that it could be changed at any time is itself what stops downstream users from trusting it. The cost is symmetric: credibility comes from irrevocability, and irrevocability breaks the chain of accountability.

Cases · Cross-National Comparison
  • Independent central banks: New Zealand legislated inflation targeting in 1989, and the model was widely copied. The delegation is narrow (one observable target), so accountability has a substitute — whether the target was met is publicly verifiable.
  • Expert cabinets in a crisis: during the 2011 euro-area debt crisis, both Italy and Greece were led for a time by unelected technocratic prime ministers. The widest delegation, the shortest tenure, the most contested afterwards — because it touches the question of who authorised you.
  • Courts as a contrast: also non-majoritarian, but their legitimacy rests on procedure (reasoned public judgments, appeal) rather than on hitting a target.

Fiscal councils and audit bodies do the opposite: they assess but decide nothing, trading effectiveness for the least contested legitimacy. The wider the delegation, the thinner the legitimacy.

The Argument

The delegation case: wherever costs fall now and benefits fall later, electoral incentives lean toward deferral. Handing the decision to a body that need not seek re-election is really a voter's pre-commitment — the equivalent of putting the alarm clock across the room.

The democratic-control case: every real policy decision has distributive consequences, and those are not technical questions. Experts can agree within a method, but choosing the objective function and deciding who absorbs the adjustment costs is a value judgement; dressing it up as technical removes the most debatable part from debate.

The trade-off: one sacrifices immediate accountability for consistency over time; the other reverses it.

Common Misreadings

"Technocracy is neutral" — methods can be neutral, objectives cannot; where you set the relative weight on two conflicting goals is itself a distributive position. "Independent means unconstrained" — independence usually means instrument independence (how), not goal independence (what).

In one line: delegating to experts is less an admission that experts are smarter than an admission that decision-makers do not trust their own future selves. Question: which decisions would you hand to a body you cannot vote out? Give the criterion, not the list.

3. Direct Democracy: Referendums and Sortition Solve Different ProblemsDirect Democracy Experiments

The Mechanism

Representative government leaves two distinct gaps: distorted preference transmission (one vote bundles hundreds of issues into a single ballot) and missing deliberation (voters lack the time and information to dig in). Two routes each address one of them. A referendum is aggregative: everyone votes on a single question, which fixes the bundling problem but exposes the result to differences in campaign spending. A citizens' assembly by lot is deliberative: dozens to hundreds of randomly selected people get weeks of time and expert hearings, which fixes information and depth but not authorisation — they carry no electoral mandate.

Only three design variables matter: who sets the question, who takes part, and whether the conclusion binds.

Cases · Cross-National Comparison
  • Switzerland: roughly 670 federal votes between 1848 and 2022, with average turnout around 45%; citizen-initiated constitutional initiatives pass only about 11% of the time. The low pass rate is really an agenda effect — an initiative forces parliament to table a counter-proposal early.
  • Ireland (the relay): a 99-member citizens' assembly deliberated on the Eighth Amendment in 2016–2017, and the 2018 referendum carried the amendment 66.4% to 33.6% — the assembly clarified the options, the referendum supplied the mandate.
  • France and Iceland (deliberation without an exit): France's 2019–2020 Citizens' Convention on Climate produced 149 proposals from 150 randomly selected citizens, and the referendum it called for was never held; Iceland's citizen-drafted constitution won about 67% in a 2012 consultative vote but never took effect, since amendment requires passage by two successive parliaments.
  • Belgium's German-speaking Community: in 2019 it created a permanent citizens' council of 24 members drawn by lot for 18-month terms, whose only job is setting the agenda; Porto Alegre in Brazil has let residents' assemblies rank part of the municipal investment budget since 1989, a practice now found in more than twenty countries.

The OECD database now holds 733 such cases, the vast majority of them advisory. The more technical and reversible the question, the better it travels; the closer to basic rights, the worse.

The Argument

The expansion case: the distortion is structural — a legislator's first incentive is re-election, not thinking an issue through. People chosen by lot face no re-election pressure, and their conclusions diverge from their own starting positions far more than ordinary polling does, which suggests deliberation genuinely changes judgement.

The case for representation (the Madisonian logic): representation is a deliberate filter. It protects unpopular minorities, who fare worst under direct majority votes; it allows cross-issue bargains — conceding A to win B, which is how real majorities are assembled and which single-issue voting cannot do; and it supplies continuing, attributable responsibility, since once an assembly disbands nobody answers for the outcome.

The trade-off: one trades certainty of accountability for quality of judgement; the other reverses it.

Common Misreadings

"A referendum result is the public will" — the outcome depends heavily on who sets the question, how it is worded, and when it is put, so who holds the power of proposal often matters more than who holds the vote. "Sortition is just grabbing people at random" — it is stratified random selection: after a mass random invitation, participants are sampled by age, region and education to approximate the population, correcting precisely for the bias that volunteering introduces.

In one line: referendums and citizens' assemblies are not two versions of the same thing — they patch two different holes in representative government, authorisation and deliberation. Question: if a citizens' assembly reaches the opposite conclusion to the latest poll, which would you believe?

4. Governance Innovation: Treating Institutions as TestableGovernance Innovation

The Mechanism

Traditional lawmaking assumes a rule stays valid long after passage, so everything must be thought through in advance. When change outpaces the legislative cycle, that assumption fails. The shared idea behind governance innovation is to write uncertainty into the design: replace one-shot general decisions with experiments bounded in scope and time. Besides pilots and controlled evaluation, three tools recur:

  • Regulatory sandboxes: a few firms may trial a business with no settled rules, under supervision and with caps on customers and duration, so real data sets the rule. Britain's Financial Conduct Authority launched the first in 2016; it has since spread to sixty-odd jurisdictions.
  • Sunset clauses: legislation carries its own expiry date and lapses unless renewed — shifting the burden of proof for continuing from opponents to supporters.
  • Digital government: Estonia's X-Road, live since 2001, carries cross-agency data requests and implements the "once-only principle": a citizen submits the same document once.

The sandbox's cost deserves stating plainly: entrants receive compliance exemptions that non-entrants do not, which sits awkwardly with formal equality before the law, and dense interaction between a regulator and a handful of firms is exactly the condition under which regulatory capture arises. Digital government is no different — the once-only principle dismantles the privacy barrier that inter-agency friction had unintentionally provided, and Estonia's compensation is to substitute auditability for inaccessibility.

The Argument

The iteration case: without trials there is no evidence, and legislation without evidence is guesswork. Because amending law is politically expensive, rules with no built-in correction mechanism tend to survive long after they stop working.

The stability case: a large part of law's value comes precisely from not changing — predictability is what lets people invest long-term and sign long contracts. An institution that iterates constantly may lower total welfare through unpredictability even if its average decision is better; and rules governing property, procedure and basic rights are the worst candidates for split testing, since a trial means contemporaries living under different rules.

Common Misreadings

"Digitisation equals better governance" — moving a redundant process online only makes a bad process run faster; improvement comes from redesigning the process and from the legal authority to share data across agencies. "Innovation equals deregulation" — sandboxes and sunset clauses are also tightening tools: they make rules easier to withdraw.

In one line: what governance innovation actually delivers is not new technology but a lower cost of correction — bad rules become easier to spot and to withdraw. Question: in a field you know well, which rule would most people be relieved to see carry a five-year sunset clause?

Going Deeper

1. Why do almost all citizen-deliberation experiments stop at "advisory"?
Because authorisation is the scarce resource. Making a small body chosen by lot binding means transferring decision power away from institutions that hold an electoral mandate — and those institutions are the only ones able to make that transfer. Ireland's answer is division of labour, not replacement: the assembly clarifies the options and leaves the mandate to a referendum or parliament. It works precisely because it does not challenge the source of authority.
2. Technocracy and direct democracy point in opposite directions — why do they often grow together?
They answer two halves of the same diagnosis: representative bodies are doing the job badly. One says decisions are too complex, give them to people who understand them; the other says decisions have been monopolised, give them back to everyone. Both come at the expense of the intermediate layer, so both can expand at once — until they collide head-on: when expert conclusions and referendum results disagree, who yields? There is no institutionalised answer.
3. If checks exist to make bad decisions hard, does every speed-up weaken them?
Not necessarily, but two kinds of friction must be separated. Informational friction — multiple rounds of review, public consultation, judicial scrutiny — exists to catch errors, and removing it does lower quality. Pure transaction cost — resubmitting the same documents, agencies passing the file around — produces no scrutiny and only consumes resources. The two look identical on a flowchart, so the test is to ask what this step has ever stopped. No answer, and it may genuinely be redundant; an answer, and it is a check.
